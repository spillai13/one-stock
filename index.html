Why it works this way

The questions people ask first, answered plainly.

Why one stock?

Spread the money across twenty stocks and you've built a small index fund: you get roughly the average. The idea here is the opposite. Put everything in the single stock with the best shot at the most growth in the least time. If it's the right one, that beats the average by a lot. The catch: one stock swings much harder than many, both ways.

Why not just buy an index fund?

For your savings, you should. An index fund gets you the market's average, the grey line on the chart. One stock, if it's the right one, gets you far more than the average. Whether the rules can find it often enough is what this experiment tests. If the blue line can't beat the grey one, the index fund wins, and this page will say so.

Why every day?

It checks every day; it doesn't trade every day. Most days the answer is "keep". Time matters as much as the gain: 10% in two weeks is worth far more than 10% in a year, because the money is free for the next pick sooner. So each morning it asks: is this still the best use of the money? If a better one shows up, it sells and switches.

Why public?

Anyone can explain yesterday's winners. The only honest test is calling them first. Every pick is written down at 10am ET, before the day's outcome is known, and stays on this page. Wins and losses sit side by side, and the record can't be quietly rewritten.

Why not just test it on past data?

Look back at any chart and the winners are obvious. Looking forward, almost nobody can tell. Most strategies that look smart on paper do worse than simply buying the whole market.

Why sell at a set price?

Before it buys, it sets the price that cuts the loss (8% below what it paid). Close below that and it's sold, no hoping it comes back. One bad stock can't sink the whole experiment, and nobody gets to change the rule after the fact.

How one stock gets picked

Trading strategies compete every trading day. Only a winner gets to pick.

01

Many strategies, one race

Each is a different way of choosing stocks. All of them are measured against the S&P 500.

02

Most lose

Beating the market once is luck. A strategy has to keep winning.

03

Only winners pick

Stop beating the market, and a strategy stops picking. It can happen any day.

04

All in on one

From the winners' picks, it puts everything in one stock and holds it until its rules say sell. No winner? It buys nothing.

The fine print, in plain words
  • No real money is involved yet. Trades are pretend: bought and sold at the day's closing price, by the rules.
  • Results don't include trading costs, which would take roughly off each buy-and-sell.
  • Until at least trades have finished, the page says "too early to tell" instead of guessing. Averages come with a range showing how sure the result is.
  • The research counts are re-checked every day and change; the date they're from is shown next to them. "In the race" means the strategies being tested now, not every one ever tried.
  • "Beat the market" means: in past trades, by more than luck usually explains, on a strict statistical test. The past doesn't promise the future. That's what this experiment checks.
